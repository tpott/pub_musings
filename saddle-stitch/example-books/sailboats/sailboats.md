## 1 — Anatomy & what a keel does

*Figure: a hull in side view (bow right) labelling bow/stem, stern/transom, the dashed waterline, draft, freeboard, the keel, and the rudder.*

The sailboat reduced to its lines. The bow points right; the dashed line is the waterline. Above it, *freeboard* keeps water out; below it, the *underbody* makes drag and lift. Two appendages do the work. The *keel* resists leeway (sideways slip) and carries ballast low — heavy weight down deep gives righting moment, the force that stands a heeled boat back up. The *rudder* steers. Every keel trades three things: *draft* (how deep you can anchor), *wetted area* (surface, so drag and speed), and *ballast depth* (stability per pound). No shape wins all three; the next pages show how designers choose.

## 2 — Full & encapsulated keels

*Figure: a full keel running the whole underbody with a keel-hung rudder; beside it, ballast moulded inside the hull laminate with no keel bolts.*

The *full keel* runs the whole underbody in one fin, rudder hung off its trailing edge. Long, shallow, forgiving: it tracks straight, takes the ground upright, and shields rudder and prop. The price is wetted area — drag — so it is slow to accelerate and turn. The classic heavy cruiser. An *encapsulated keel* molds the ballast inside the hull laminate instead of bolting on an external lump: no keel bolts to corrode or shear, and a grounding is absorbed by solid glass. Kraken Yachts build a full-length encapsulated "Zero Keel," sold as the safest way to carry ballast offshore — at the cost of the same full-keel drag and harder repairs.

## 3 — Fin, bulb & wing keels

*Figure: three short keels side by side — a plain deep fin, a fin with a torpedo bulb at the tip, and a shortened fin with horizontal winglets.*

The modern *fin keel* concentrates ballast and lift into a short, deep bolted blade. Less wetted area means more speed and eager turning, and depth puts ballast low for stability — but draft limits anchorages and the spade rudder behind it is exposed. A *bulb keel* hangs a torpedo of ballast at the fin's tip, dropping the weight as low as possible so less ballast does more; the bulb adds a little drag and snags weed. A *wing keel* adds horizontal winglets to a shortened fin: shoal draft that still stands up, loved for thin water — though the wings bury in mud on a hard grounding.

## 4 — Shoal-draft & exotic keels

*Figure: four options — bilge (twin) keels drying out level (bow-on), a pivoting centerboard, a ballasted lifting keel in its trunk, and a canting keel swung to windward with a daggerboard (bow-on).*

When water is shallow, ballast moves or lifts. *Bilge (twin) keels* sit low on each side so the boat dries out level on her own legs at low tide — at the cost of extra wetted area. A *centerboard* pivots down from a case (raised, the boat floats in inches); a *daggerboard* drops straight instead, and so cannot kick up on grounding. A *lifting keel* hauls a full ballasted keel up into a trunk for shoal or trailerable draft. A *canting keel* swings its bulb to windward for huge righting moment, adding daggerboards to make lift — pure racing complexity.

## 5 — Comfort ratio, by overlay

*Figure: two hull profiles drawn to the same waterline length and superimposed — a heavy, deep, fine-ended passagemaker at full opacity, with a flatter, shallower, beamier light cruiser laid over it at ~40% opacity.*

Ted Brewer's Comfort Ratio estimates how gently a hull moves in a seaway: *CR = displacement ÷ (0.65 × (0.7·LWL + 0.3·LOA) × beam^1.333)*. Heavy, narrow, long-waterline boats score high and roll slowly; light, beamy boats score low and snap back. To *see* it rather than compute it, overlay two profiles drawn to the same waterline length: render one at full opacity, drop the other to about 40 percent, then lay the faint one over the solid one. The differences jump out — the heavy passagemaker (solid) carries more displacement below the line with finer ends; the light cruiser (faint) is flatter and beamier. Same length, very different motion.

## 6 — Rudders: how they're hung

*Figure: four rudders from exposed to protected — transom-hung on pintles, a cantilevered spade, a skeg-hung blade behind a fixed fin, and a keel-hung rudder on a full keel.*

A rudder steers by deflecting water flow, so it only bites when water moves past it — from boatspeed or prop wash. How it is hung sets the trade between steering power and toughness, from exposed to protected. A *transom-hung* rudder bolts to the stern on pintles, all outside and easy to service. A *spade* cantilevers from its stock alone: light, powerful, efficient — and fully exposed to grounding and debris. A *skeg-hung* rudder sits behind a fixed fin that supports and shields it: the cruiser's compromise. A *keel-hung* rudder hinges along a full keel's trailing edge: the most protected, but heavy and slow on the helm.

## 7 — The skeg, and helm balance

*Figure: a skeg detail (fixed fin giving the rudder a lower bearing) above a comparison of an unbalanced blade — all area aft of the stock — against a balanced blade whose area straddles the stock.*

The skeg is a fixed fin ahead of the rudder: it adds a lower bearing and a leading edge that fends off lines and nets, so the boat keeps steering even if the blade is damaged. Separately, where the stock crosses the blade sets helm feel. An *unbalanced* rudder has all its area aft of the stock, so pressure sits behind the pivot and the helm feels heavy. A *balanced* rudder shifts part of the area forward of the stock so the loads partly cancel — lighter and quicker. Too much, and it turns twitchy.

## 8 — Prop wash & prop walk

*Figure: an accelerated cone of water leaving the propeller and washing across the rudder; below, a top view of the stern walking to port in reverse.*

A propeller throws a fast, twisting column of water astern — the *prop wash*. In forward gear it blasts onto the rudder, which is why a burst of throttle gives steering even at a standstill: the wash supplies the flow the rudder needs. Skippers "kick" the throttle while turning the wheel to pivot in tight quarters. The same spin has a side effect — *prop walk*: a right-handed prop pushes the stern to starboard ahead and to port astern. Backing to port, the stern tucks toward a port-side dock — nuisance becomes docking tool.
