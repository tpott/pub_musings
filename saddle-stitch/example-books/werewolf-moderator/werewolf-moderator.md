# The Moderator's Grimoire

*Running Werewolf &amp; Mafia — a pocket field guide for the narrator*

## 1 — The game in one picture

*Figure: an informed minority (two shaded heads) hidden in an uninformed majority (seven outlined heads), all inside the dashed ring of "the village."*

Werewolf and Mafia are one game: an **informed minority** (the wolves, who secretly know each other) hidden inside an **uninformed majority** (the villagers, who don't). Dmitry Davidoff built it as a psychology exercise at Moscow State University in 1987; Andrew Plotkin re-themed it as Werewolf in 1997. Play alternates **NIGHT** — the wolves secretly kill one villager — and **DAY** — everyone debates and votes one player out. The **village wins** when every wolf is gone. The **wolves win at parity**: the moment they equal the remaining non-wolves, they can no longer be out-voted.

**How many wolves?** Balance is a dial, not a fixed number. A common rule of thumb is **one wolf per four players, rounded up**; Plotkin's simulations add the third wolf only around seventeen. Braverman, Etesami &amp; Mossel proved the dial's shape: with **no Seer** the sides are even when wolves ≈ **√(players)**; **with a Seer** the wolves must hold a fixed share of the table. Real games settle on **2–3 wolves for 8–15 players**; if roles aren't revealed on death the village loses information, so run thinner — one wolf per five. Everything else in this booklet is flavor and dials on this one engine.

## 2 — Setup: pick a moderator, deal the deck

*Figure: nine face-up role cards in a row — two Wolves, one Seer, six Villagers — the minimal deck.*

Choose a neutral **moderator** — you — who runs the game but plays no side. **Player counts in this booklet never include the moderator:** "nine players" means nine dealt cards plus you, ten people in the room. Seven players is a workable floor, **nine to eleven is the sweet spot.** Seat everyone in a circle so you can see all faces at night. The classic nine-player deck is **2 Werewolves, 1 Seer, 6 Villagers** — one card each, dealt face-down; each player peeks and keeps it secret.

No printed deck is required. A standard 52-card deck works (the two black jacks are wolves, the queen of hearts the Seer, anything else a villager); so do labeled scraps of paper in a hat. With no props at all, walk the circle with every eye closed and assign roles by a whisper or a tap on the shoulder.

Settle the **tie rule before you deal**, so nobody litigates it mid-vote: a tied vote goes to a run-off among the tied players only, and if that ties again nobody is eliminated and the day is lost. A **Mayor**, whose vote counts double, breaks most ties outright (page 4). Announce two more things before dealing: whether a dead player's card is revealed, and whether the game ends at parity or plays to the last body.

## 3 — The loop — and how it starts

*Figure: a three-node cycle — NIGHT → DAWN → DAY — with an arrow marking "start here (night 0)."*

The game opens at night, not day. "Everyone, close your eyes" starts **Night 0**, when the one-time roles fire: the wolves open their eyes and learn each other, Cupid ties the Lovers, Masons meet. Many groups let the wolves kill on Night 0; gentler tables make it **meet-only**, so the first day opens with no body and the table gets one round of introductions before the stakes rise. Either way, **the first day starts when you say "everyone, open your eyes" and announce what dawn brought** — a death, or a quiet night.

From there, three beats cycle until someone wins. **NIGHT:** eyes closed; the wolves and special roles act in secret. **DAWN:** you resolve the night privately — protections, then kills, then triggers like a Hunter's dying shot. **DAY:** you announce the dead, and the living discuss, accuse, and vote one player out (next page). Then night falls again. On later nights only the recurring actions repeat — the one-time roles never fire twice.

## 4 — Day: accuse, defend, vote

*Figure: six heads in a row, accusation arrows converging on one, with a tally of votes beneath it.*

"Open your eyes; it's morning." Announce the night's deaths (and roles, if that's your policy). The **dead are out** — no speaking, gesturing, or reacting. **Time-box the day out loud**: five minutes in a small game, ten in a large one. Nobody is tried until an accusation is seconded.

1. **Accusation &amp; second** — anyone living may accuse anyone living, but the accusation dies unless a second player seconds it. **No second, no trial**: discussion resumes.
2. **The case** — the accuser, then the seconder, each give one short reason the accused is a wolf.
3. **Defense** — the accused answers, uninterrupted.
4. **Open floor** — the table questions the accused, but **the accused chooses who speaks**; two or three questions is plenty.
5. **Last words** — one closing line from the accused, then silence.
6. **Vote** — simultaneous hands on "three — two — one," so nobody waits to see the bandwagon. A majority eliminates; ties go to the run-off you announced (page 2).

Then read the result. **Someone voted out**, or **the clock run out** — either way, night falls. **Otherwise** the day carries on, and anyone may raise a fresh accusation.

## 5 — Night: eyes closed, the wake-order

*Figure: a numbered vertical list of night roles, each with an eye — Cupid, Bodyguard, Werewolves, Little Girl, Seer, Witch.*

"Everyone, close your eyes." Players **hum or tap** to mask the sound of pointing. Then call each night role in a **fixed order**, one at a time: it opens its eyes, silently points at a target, closes them again. A classic order: **Cupid** (first night) → **Bodyguard/Doctor** protects → **Werewolves** choose a victim → the **Little Girl** may risk a peek → **Seer** looks (you answer with a silent thumb, wolf or not) → **Witch**, who may heal or poison. Keep every action silent, keep your phrasing identical each night, and keep "waking" dead roles so the living can't tell which powers remain.

**On Zoom or Meet**, typing replaces pointing and there is nothing to mask — skip the humming. Deal roles by private message before the first night. At night, everyone mutes and turns cameras off while you narrate; the wolves agree on a victim in a private group chat (a Discord thread works), and the Seer messages you a name — you answer the same way. By day, cameras come back on and the dead stay muted. Companion apps (werewolv.es, Wolvesville, the One Night app) will run the night for you.

## 6 — Dawn: resolving the night

*Figure: two outcomes — a target under a shield survives (✓); an unshielded target dies (✗) and a Hunter's dying arrow drags another down.*

Between the last night action and the morning announcement, quietly settle what actually happened. Work the same three steps every dawn, **in this order** — the order is what makes the outcome fair:

1. **Protections** — if the **Doctor** or **Bodyguard** covered the wolves' target, the attack fails. Announce it as "someone was attacked but survived," and nobody dies.
2. **Kills** — apply every attack that landed: the wolves' victim, plus any independent killer (a **Serial Killer**, the **Witch**'s poison).
3. **Triggers** — deaths that cause deaths. A **Hunter** shot on death drags one player down with him; the **Lovers** die together; a **Cursed** villager attacked by the wolves *turns into a wolf* instead of dying.

Only once the ledger settles do you open the day and name the dead — and name them *in character*. Pick your scenario from the table on page 11 and steal its dawn lines from pages 12–14: "the river gave him back at dawn, shoes full of cement" seeds a day of suspicion in a way that "Tony is dead" never will.

## 7 — Winning: parity and the endgame

*Figure: a balance beam — two wolves on one pan level with two villagers on the other — labelled "parity → wolves win."*

Three win conditions sit on the table at once. The **village** wins the instant the last wolf (and any third-party killer) is gone. The **wolves** win at **parity** — when wolves equal the non-wolves, because they can no longer be out-voted. **Neutrals** have private conditions: the Tanner wins only if voted out; the Serial Killer or Piper wins by being last standing or by charming everyone. Davidoff's original declared the mafia the winner the moment they equaled the innocents; many modern groups instead play until a whole faction is eliminated, because it's easier to explain.

## 8 — The village: informers, protectors, trust

*Figure: a Seer at a crystal ball points at a target head; the moderator answers with a silent ✓ / ✗.*

The village's power is **information**; its armor, **protection**; its glue, **confirmed trust**. The starter kit:

- **Seer** (Fortune Teller, Cop) — points at one player a night; you silently signal wolf or not. The wolves' prime target once revealed; an **Apprentice Seer** can inherit the job.
- **Doctor / Bodyguard** (Healer, Guardian) — protects one player a night ("someone was attacked but survived"); usually can't guard the same target twice running.
- **Hunter** — when killed, by night or by vote, immediately drags one player down with him.
- **Witch** — two one-shot potions, one heal and one poison; best hoarded for the endgame.
- **Cupid &amp; the Lovers** — first night only: two players are tied together; if one dies, both die. A wolf–human pair can win as a couple.
- **Masons** — wake once to learn each other are innocent: an anchor of trust.
- **Mayor** (Sheriff) — votes double; breaks tied days.
- **Little Girl** — may peek during the wolves' turn, but dies in the victim's place if caught.

Take one or two, not eight. Give the village too much intel and the wolves have nowhere left to hide.

## 9 — Wolf &amp; mafia roles

*Figure: a Godfather — a dark wolf behind a white "innocent" mask — reads clean to the Seer's eye.*

The pack has its own toolbox:

- **Werewolves** (Mafiosi, Goons) — wake together each night and agree on one victim.
- **Godfather** — reads as *innocent* to the Seer; the signature mafia deceiver.
- **Minion** — knows the wolves and wins with them but isn't one, so the wolves never eat him and the Seer clears him.
- **Sorcerer** — an evil Seer, hunting the real Seer to expose them.
- **Wolf Cub** — if killed, the pack takes two kills the next night.
- **Cursed** — a villager who *becomes a wolf* when attacked, instead of dying.
- **Janitor** — hides a victim's role to deny the village its clue.

## 10 — Neutral &amp; third-party roles

*Figure: a Tanner in a jester's cap grinning as votes point at him; a lone Serial Killer standing apart.*

Some players answer to no faction. Each carries a private win condition, and each one bends the table around it:

- **Tanner** (Jester, Fool) — wins *only if the village votes him out*, so he acts guilty on purpose and warps every vote.
- **Serial Killer** — kills one player a night, is often immune to attack, and wins by being the last one alive: the hardest role in the game.
- **Arsonist** — douses players over several nights, then ignites them all at once, bypassing every heal.
- **Survivor** — needs only to be alive at the end, so he sides with whoever is winning.
- **Piper** — charms two players a night and wins the moment every living player is charmed.

One neutral is plenty; two turn a hunt into a free-for-all. And say so out loud before dealing, because a neutral killer moves the goalposts — the village cannot win until *every* third-party killer is gone, not just the wolves (page 7).

## 11 — Scenarios: one skeleton, many costumes

*Figure: a mapping table — the same six functions across six scenarios.*

A **scenario** changes nothing under the hood. Only the **flavor** moves: of the killers, the investigator, the protector, and "death." The wolves become mafia, outlaws, mutineers, zombies, or impostors; the **Seer** becomes a detective, a sheriff, a lookout, a scientist, a scanner; "death" becomes whacked, shot, thrown overboard, devoured, ejected. Every column below is the same game. **Bang!** (2002) is a published Western cousin of the same engine.

Because the rules don't move, you can dress a game in your group's favorite setting **without rebalancing a thing** — a scenario is not a variant, and it costs you nothing. What it does cost is *your mouth*: a scenario lives or dies in how you narrate each dawn and seed each day. The next three pages are narration ammunition for six of them.

| Killers | Werewolves | Mafia | Outlaws | Mutineers | Zombies | Impostors |

## 12 — Running the scenario: mafia &amp; western

Each scenario needs three lines from you, in the order you will use them: a **first-morning prompt** that drops the table into character before anyone has spoken, a **dawn kill**, and a **dawn save** for the mornings a protector blocked the hit.

**Mafia: the neighborhood** — rain, neon, nobody saw anything.

- *First morning:* "The Family has moved into the neighborhood, and someone at this table takes their coffee with them. Who's new in town — and who vouches for whom?"
- *Kill:* "The town woke to sirens. Tony never made it home — the river gave him back at dawn, shoes full of cement."
- *Save:* "Shots rang out on Fifth Street, but the bullet only found the doorframe. Everyone lived."

**Western: the frontier town** — dust, spurs, a too-quiet saloon.

- *First morning:* "Strangers stepped off the stagecoach this week. The sheriff wants everyone's alibi for last night — starting with yours."
- *Kill:* "Sun came up over the corral and found Big Jim face-down in it, six-shooter cold, boots still on."
- *Save:* "Lead flew in the night — but Doc dug the slug out by lamplight. She'll ride by noon."

## 13 — Running the scenario: pirates &amp; zombies

**Pirates: mutiny aboard** — creaking rigging, a crew that counts its knives.

- *First morning:* "The captain's compass is gone and the powder's been moved. There's mutiny aboard — who stood watch, and who will swear to it?"
- *Kill:* "The sea lay flat calm all night — and still the bosun went over the rail. No storm did that."
- *Save:* "Steel flashed below decks, but the ship's surgeon stitched fast. He'll live to haul rope."

**Zombies: the last camp** — a boarded-up camp, a generator on its last can of fuel.

- *First morning:* "The latch was lifted from the inside. One of us is already bitten and hiding it. Who was near the gate after dark?"
- *Kill:* "At sunup, Maria's bunk was empty. The fence gate hung open, and the drag marks led out — not in."
- *Save:* "The fever hit Old Sam at midnight, but the medic's last dose held. By morning his eyes were clear."

Deaths in character keep the dread alive; saves in character make the protector feel mighty without ever naming them.

## 14 — Running the scenario: space &amp; fairy tale

**Space: the crew and the impostor** — humming vents, a crew badge that doesn't scan.

- *First morning:* "Sensors logged movement in the vents while every bunk read occupied. Run the numbers: one of these badges is forged."
- *Kill:* "The airlock cycled at 03:00, ship's time. Kai's suit is still on its hook. Kai isn't."
- *Save:* "Life support dipped in the night, but the engineer rerouted the O2 before anyone woke."

**Fairy tale: the fox in the henhouse** — a sunny farmyard where nothing ever really dies; built for the kids' table (next page).

- *First morning:* "Someone in this farmyard has a bushy tail tucked under their coat. Who was awake when the moon was up?"
- *"Kill":* "This morning one nest was empty, feathers by the gate — Henrietta has been led off to nap in the fox's den until we catch him."
- *Save:* "The old sheepdog slept by the coop last night, and the fox went home hungry."

## 15 — For kids: sleep, not death — and when

*Figure: a Sandman sprinkles sleep; a villager rests head-down with a "Zzz" — asleep, not dead.*

The barrier to family play is the **violence, not the logic.** So recast death as **sleep**: instead of a killer, a **Sandman** puts players "to sleep" — head down, eyes closed — and a counter-role can wake them. The eliminated player is napping, not dead (the fox's den, previous page), which keeps the mechanics intact while removing the blood.

**When are they ready?** Developmental research (Kang Lee's "peeking game" studies) finds most children can *tell* a lie by four — but *sustaining* one under questioning leans on second-order theory of mind ("she thinks that I think…") that matures around **seven or eight**. The game boxes agree: One Night is commonly rated 8+, Miller's Hollow 10+, big-table Ultimate Werewolf 13+, and Among Us is rated E10+ by the ESRB (PEGI 7). So from about five, a child can hold a secret role in a short, gentle round; from seven or eight they can bluff under cross-examination — and love it.

**Simplify hard:** wolves and villagers only (add a Seer once the first game lands); an adult narrator; days capped at two or three minutes; fantastical themes over mafia. Above all keep **every child in the game** — run short back-to-back rounds, or make the just-eliminated kid your co-narrator.

## 16 — One Night: no moderator, ten minutes

*Figure: a single NIGHT block and a single DAY-with-one-vote block; a swap arrow between two cards; "no elimination."*

**One Night Ultimate Werewolf** (Bezier Games, 2014) compresses the whole game into a single night and a single day — about ten minutes, 3–10 players, **no elimination and no moderator**: a player-Announcer runs the script with eyes closed, or the free app narrates. Deal one card per player and **always three more to the center**, so any role may be in nobody's hands. The night churns cards instead of killing: the **Werewolves** find each other (a lone wolf may peek at one center card); the **Seer** views one player's card *or two* center cards; the **Robber** swaps his card with a player's and peeks at his new self; the **Troublemaker** swaps two *other* players' cards blind; the **Drunk** must trade with the center, no peeking; the **Insomniac** wakes last to re-check what she now is.

Day is a few minutes of debate, then one simultaneous vote on "three–two–one." Everyone tied for most votes dies; if no one takes two votes, no one dies. **You are whatever card sits in front of you at dawn** — swapped players win and die as their new role. The village wins if a wolf dies, or if there are no wolves and no one dies; the **Tanner** wins only by dying. Great with kids — but these swap roles are this variant's alone; never mix them into a classic multi-round game.

## 17 — The moderator's craft

*Figure: the narrator at the center of the circle with a script, still "waking" a dead role so the living can't tell what's left.*

You are the neutral engine, and your discipline is the game's quality. Keep a **fixed script** and identical phrasing ("Werewolves, open your eyes") so word-order leaks nothing. **Talk toward the center** of the circle so no one hears who you face. The single most important rule: **the dead stay silent** — and you must not correct even their misremembered history. Keep **"waking" dead special roles** anyway ("Seer, open your eyes") so the living can't deduce which powers remain. Add only **one or two** special roles per game; more roles is not more fun.

## 18 — Reveal-on-death &amp; balancing

*Figure: two cards — one face-up (reveal, 3:1) and one face-down (no-reveal, 4–5:1) — the ratio you owe for each choice.*

Your biggest early choice is whether to show an eliminated player's card. **Reveal-on-death** hands the village a powerful information engine — the default for new and casual groups; pay for it with an extra wolf or fewer village powers. **No-reveal** (Davidoff's original) is harder and swingier, so lean the ratio to **4:1 or 5:1**. Whatever you pick, announce it *before* dealing. To balance a deck, start from one your group knows and **change one thing at a time**; counter a strong **Seer** with a **Sorcerer** or an extra wolf, and blunt a strong pack by cutting a wolf or adding a **Tanner** to muddy every vote. **Playtest — psychology beats math.**
